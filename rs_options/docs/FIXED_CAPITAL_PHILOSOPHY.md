# Evidence-Based Fixed-Capital Stock-Trading Philosophy

**Prepared by Manus AI — August 27, 2026**

> **Financial disclaimer:** I’m an AI, not a licensed financial advisor. This is analysis, not guaranteed advice; investing and trading carry risks you bear. A $26 account can be lost entirely, and no process can make trading foolproof or reliably produce 20% per trade.

## Executive conclusion

This plan is designed for a U.S. Robinhood account beginning with approximately **$26**, with **no additional money ever added**, likely fractional-share access, and permission to hold positions overnight. The account is therefore a fixed-capital experiment. The objective is to pursue growth toward milestones such as $100 while preserving the ability to continue trading and measuring whether a repeatable process exists.

The central philosophy is:

> **No money will be added to the account. Capital must be preserved, and growth must come only from the account’s realized trading and investment results. The 1% risk rule is waived because it is operationally too small at $26, but it is replaced by an absolute dollar-loss ceiling, strict instrument filters, one-position concentration limits, and mandatory no-trade decisions.**

A 20% return on every trade remains an invalid objective. It creates a quota that encourages overtrading, oversized positions, illiquid securities, revenge trading, and account ruin. The correct objective is to maximize **positive expectancy after spread, slippage, fees, taxes where applicable, and execution error**, while keeping the account alive long enough to learn.

There are three valid decisions: **trade long, trade short only if separately authorized and understood, or do not trade**. For this account, the default should be long-only equities or fractional equities. “No trade” is a successful decision when the edge, liquidity, or risk cannot be validated.

## 1. The new fixed-capital doctrine

The original plan treated risk as a percentage of equity. That principle is sound for a sufficiently large account, but 1% of $26 is only $0.26, which is below the practical minimum risk capacity of many trades. Applying the rule mechanically can make every trade impossible; waiving it entirely can make every trade dangerous.

The revised doctrine is therefore:

1. **No deposits.** The account balance may increase only through realized trading or investment results. Depositing more money is not part of the plan.
2. **No leverage or margin.** Borrowing is incompatible with a fixed-capital survival experiment.
3. **No options.** A standard option contract can represent 100 shares, and the entire premium can be lost.[2]
4. **One position at a time.** There is no diversification benefit when the account is too small to size multiple positions safely.
5. **Absolute loss limits replace the 1% rule.** Percentage risk may be higher, but dollar risk remains bounded.
6. **No forced trades.** A daily return quota is prohibited.
7. **No sub-$1 or OTC stocks.** Low nominal price is not the same as low risk or low cost.
8. **No averaging down.** Additions to a losing position are prohibited unless a separate, validated rule eventually permits them.
9. **No overnight binary-event exposure.** Earnings, FDA decisions, merger votes, and similar events are excluded during validation.
10. **Growth is secondary to survival and evidence.** A lucky doubling is not proof that the strategy works.

## 2. What “same result” means

The desired broad outcome is assumed to be growing approximately $26 to $100 and potentially beyond. The original all-win illustration—20% on every trade—is mathematically possible only under unrealistic assumptions and ignores losses, spreads, slippage, taxes, liquidity, and changing market conditions.

The revised definition of success is more demanding and more useful:

| Dimension | Success definition |
|---|---|
| Capital | Account reaches the milestone without a ruin event |
| Process | At least 90%–95% adherence to written rules during validation |
| Performance | Positive expectancy after realistic costs |
| Robustness | Results survive multiple market regimes and out-of-sample testing |
| Risk | Drawdowns and loss streaks remain below predefined limits |
| Execution | Actual fills and exits are practical at the account’s size |
| Behavior | No revenge trading, rule changes after entry, or forced activity |

A strategy that reaches $100 through one 200% speculative move has produced an outcome, not evidence of a durable edge. The account is successful only when it grows **without destroying the process that produced the growth**.

The loss ceiling is not a target and is not guaranteed. Overnight gaps, trading halts, thin liquidity, and fast markets can cause an actual loss greater than the intended stop. The account must never hold a position whose realistic stress loss could consume the entire balance.

## 4. Instrument policy

### Preferred universe: liquid fractional equities and ETFs

The preferred instrument is a liquid, exchange-listed security that can be purchased fractionally. A higher-priced stock held fractionally may be safer than a $0.30 stock because it may have better disclosure, liquidity, institutional participation, and execution quality.

| Filter | Starting requirement |
|---|---:|
| Listing | NYSE or Nasdaq; no OTC securities |
| Market capitalization | Preferably above $1 billion |
| Average dollar volume | Preferably above $25–$50 million per day |
| Spread | Preferably below 0.50% of price |
| Corporate information | Current filings and understandable business model |
| Event risk | No imminent earnings or binary announcement |
| Corporate actions | No recent reverse split, unexplained ticker change, or repeated dilution |
| Execution | Fractional order must be practical and preferably limit-based |

### Secondary universe: low-priced exchange-listed shares

A stock below $5 may be considered only as a tightly screened research satellite. It must be exchange-listed, liquid, current in its filings, free of obvious promotional behavior, and capable of being exited without materially moving the market.

Sub-$1 stocks, OTC stocks, shell companies, pre-revenue biotech names, warrants, leveraged ETFs, and promotion-driven names are prohibited. FINRA warns that low-priced securities may be volatile, thinly traded, difficult to research, and vulnerable to manipulation.[1] Investor.gov similarly describes microcap risks involving limited information, low liquidity, and manipulation.[3]

## 5. Recommended strategy: overnight liquid-equity swing trading

The most practical active strategy for this fixed account is not rapid day trading. It is a simple, rules-based swing strategy using liquid fractional equities or ETFs and a holding period of several days when the thesis remains valid.

The initial setup is:

> **Liquid-stock pullback and reclaim:** identify a security in an established daily trend; wait for a controlled pullback toward a previously identified support or moving-average zone; enter only after price reclaims the level with acceptable volume and market context; define invalidation below the recent swing low; exit at the target, invalidation, time stop, or material thesis change.

This is not claimed to be inherently profitable. It is preferred because it is easier to define, test, and execute than a rapidly changing intraday strategy. A second strategy should not be added until the first has a meaningful sample.

### Trade-plan template

Every live trade must specify all of the following before entry:

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Symbol | Exact ticker and exchange |
| Instrument | Whole or fractional equity/ETF share |
| Direction | Long only during initial validation |
| Setup | Exact strategy name |
| Entry | Price and confirmation condition |
| Invalidation | Objective price or thesis condition |
| Position size | Fractional quantity derived from risk ceiling |
| Planned loss | Dollar estimate including spread and slippage |
| Target | Price and expected reward in dollars and R |
| Time stop | Maximum holding period if price does not follow through |
| Event check | Earnings, filings, news, and corporate actions |
| Exit plan | Stop, target, time stop, and emergency condition |
| Counter-evidence | What could make the thesis wrong |

If a field is blank, the decision is no trade.

## 6. Risk and position sizing

The account uses an absolute risk ceiling instead of a mechanical 1% ceiling. For an initial trade, planned loss should normally remain around **$0.75–$1.00**. This is approximately 3%–4% of the initial account, which is aggressive, but it reflects the account’s practical minimum size. It should not be increased simply because the account has a short winning streak.

A simplified sizing calculation is:

```text
Position quantity = planned dollar risk ÷ distance from entry to invalidation
```

For fractional shares, the quantity must also respect the broker’s minimum order and fractional-share rules. If the calculated quantity is too small to execute or the position’s stress loss is too large, skip the trade.

The position should be stress-tested using at least three scenarios:

| Scenario | Question |
|---|---|
| Normal stop | What is the planned loss if price reaches invalidation? |
| Slippage | What if the exit is filled worse than expected? |
| Overnight gap | What if price opens materially beyond the stop? |

The trade is acceptable only if the gap scenario does not create an unacceptable probability of account failure. This is why thin low-priced stocks are generally unsuitable for overnight holding.

## 7. Additional tactics that improve the plan

### A. Regime filter before every trade

Classify the broad market as trending upward, trending downward, range-bound, or unclear. Trade only when the setup fits the regime. For example, do not use a breakout strategy in a choppy range, and do not use aggressive mean reversion against a strong market trend.

### B. Market-off switch

Define conditions under which no new trades are permitted: stale data, broken market connectivity, abnormal spreads, unexpected macro announcements, extreme volatility, broker errors, or two consecutive losses. The market-off switch is a feature, not a failure.

### C. Minimum expected move

Reject trades where the expected price move is too small to overcome the spread, slippage, and overnight gap risk. A trade that is “likely to make a few cents” may still have negative expectancy after friction.

### D. Time stops

A position that fails to move within the expected time window consumes capital and creates opportunity cost. Close it after a predefined number of sessions unless the thesis remains exceptionally strong and the extension is itself part of the tested rule.

### E. Profit protection without premature exits

Do not move a stop to breakeven merely because the trade is slightly profitable. Use a pre-tested trailing rule or a structural exit. Otherwise, normal noise may remove the trade before the intended move develops.

### F. Liquidity stress testing

Review the quote at normal and stressed times. Record the spread at entry, the spread near the close, and the spread during any fast movement. Reject securities whose spread becomes a material percentage of the account’s planned profit.

### G. Anti-promotion protocol

A trade may not be justified primarily by social-media posts, chat-room urgency, influencer screenshots, anonymous “alerts,” or claims of guaranteed returns. The thesis must be independently expressible in terms of market context, price structure, liquidity, catalyst, and risk.

### H. Delayed execution for copied ideas

If using another trader’s research, do not copy the order automatically. Wait for your own entry condition and compare the source trader’s actual entry, liquidity, leverage, and time horizon with yours. Copy the process, not the button press.

### I. Parameter freeze

Choose the rules before collecting the validation sample. Do not adjust the entry, stop, target, or universe after every loss. Parameter changes require a scheduled review and a fresh out-of-sample test.

### J. Separate research from live capital

Maintain a paper-trading ledger for strategies not yet validated. The live account should contain only the currently authorized setup. This prevents every new idea from becoming a real-money experiment.

## 8. Validation and evidence standards

Backtest or replay the exact written rules, not a flexible narrative. Separate development, validation, out-of-sample, and walk-forward periods. Test for look-ahead bias, survivorship bias, stale data, unrealistic fills, omitted spread, omitted slippage, multiple testing, and data leakage.

Track at least:

| Metric | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Number of trades | Establishes sample size |
| Win rate | Descriptive, not sufficient by itself |
| Average winner and loser | Determines payoff structure |
| Expectancy after costs | Primary performance measure |
| Profit factor | Compares gross wins with gross losses |
| Maximum drawdown | Measures survival risk |
| Worst day and week | Tests tail outcomes |
| Consecutive losses | Helps assess psychological and regime risk |
| Performance by regime | Detects conditional failure |
| Slippage and spread | Tests execution realism |
| Rule adherence | Separates strategy failure from operator failure |

A simple expectancy formula is:

```text
Expectancy = (win rate × average win) − (loss rate × average loss) − trading costs
```

A strategy should not be promoted based on a handful of profitable trades. A practical minimum is 50 trades for an initial review and preferably 100 or more trades before making strong claims about robustness. Use bootstrap or Monte Carlo resampling of the trade sequence to estimate plausible drawdown and loss-streak ranges.

## 9. Copying existing knowledge responsibly

Existing knowledge and experience can be valuable, but direct copy trading introduces timing differences, selection bias, hidden leverage, and misaligned incentives. A peer-reviewed *Management Science* study found that the ability to copy other traders increased risk-taking in its experimental market and concluded that copy trading led to excessive risk-taking in that setting.[5]

Use this replication checklist:

| Question | Required evidence |
|---|---|
| Is the source identifiable? | Name, history, and verifiable methodology |
| Is performance complete? | Losing periods and full history, not selected winners |
| Is the strategy reproducible? | Written entry, exit, sizing, and risk rules |
| Does it fit the account? | Same instruments, liquidity, capital, and holding period |
| Are incentives aligned? | No pressure to trade frequently or buy products |
| Is the result cost-adjusted? | Spread, slippage, fees, and taxes considered |
| Can losses be bounded? | Explicit trade, day, week, and account limits |

The strongest form of copying is to copy **decision architecture**: how the source defines a setup, filters candidates, sizes positions, handles losses, and decides not to trade.

## 10. Daily and weekly operating procedure

### Before market open

Review the broad-market context, sector strength, scheduled economic releases, earnings, corporate news, overnight gaps, and liquidity. Classify the regime. Create a watchlist of no more than five candidates, then eliminate candidates that fail the liquidity, event, or risk filters.

### Before entering a position

Complete the trade-plan template, calculate the fractional position size, estimate the normal-stop and gap losses, and confirm that the security is eligible for the account. Use a limit order where practical. Never chase a price merely because it is moving.

### While the position is open

Do not widen the stop, average down, double size after a loss, or change the thesis to avoid admitting an error. If an unexpected event invalidates the thesis, exit according to the emergency rule.

### After closing the position

Record the entry, exit, spread, slippage, holding time, realized P/L, R-multiple, rule adherence, and screenshot or quote evidence when permitted. Do not change the strategy because of one trade.

### Weekly review

Review every 10–20 trades for execution quality and every 20–50 trades for strategy evidence. Change parameters only when the evidence is meaningful and the revised rules survive out-of-sample testing.

## 12. Prohibited behaviors

The following are prohibited under this philosophy:

- Depositing additional money to rescue a losing strategy.
- Using margin, leverage, or borrowed funds.
- Trading options with the fixed account.
- Trading sub-$1, OTC, shell, promotional, or repeatedly diluted securities.
- Buying because a stock is cheap per share.
- Averaging down without a pre-tested rule.
- Moving a stop farther away.
- Doubling after losses or increasing size after a short winning streak.
- Holding through earnings or another known binary event during validation.
- Using market orders in thin securities.
- Copying an anonymous trader’s order without independent validation.
- Treating AI output as a risk-control mechanism.
- Changing the rules after seeing the result.
- Forcing a trade to satisfy a daily profit target.

### The most valuable additions

The most transferable insights are **context first, location before entry, strict invalidation, execution over P&L, good-loss/bad-loss classification, and eliminating C-game sessions**. These improve the plan without requiring specialized futures tools.

The strongest new operating rule is:

> **Do not ask only whether the setup is present. First ask whether today’s environment is suitable for the setup and whether the trader is in a state to execute it correctly.**

A setup that works in a clean trend may fail repeatedly in a range. A setup that works at the New York open may fail during a slow midday session. A technically valid setup may also be a bad trade if the spread is too wide, the account cannot size it safely, or the trader is trying to recover a prior loss.

### Forced participation as a testable hypothesis

“Forced participation” should be treated as a market-mechanics hypothesis rather than a narrative explanation applied after the fact. A valid test requires a precise definition of what counts as a failed move, what confirms that participants are trapped, how much follow-through is expected, and where the thesis is invalidated.

For this account, a simplified equity-compatible proxy is:

1. Price makes a visible break of a prior level.
2. The break fails to attract sustained continuation.
3. Price returns through the level and holds on a retest.
4. Volume is at least normal relative to the security and time of day.
5. The broader market is not contradicting the direction.
6. Entry occurs only after the retest confirms, with a predefined invalidation level.

This is a **failed-breakout/reclaim setup**, not proof that trapped traders are causing the move. The claim must be tested through a trade ledger.

### Fib levels: useful map, dangerous authority

The 0.705, 0.786, and 0.886 levels can help a discretionary trader describe where price is pulling back within a swing. They should not be treated as magical levels or universal “golden pockets.” Different swing selections create different levels, and selecting the swing after seeing the outcome creates hindsight bias.

If Fibonacci retracements are used, the swing high, swing low, timeframe, and level must be recorded before entry. Compare Fib-based trades with otherwise similar non-Fib trades. Promote the levels only if they add measurable expectancy after costs.

### Order flow and Robinhood limitations

The expert’s use of delta, bid/ask aggression, and imbalances depends on high-quality real-time market data and rapid execution. A retail brokerage chart may not provide the same depth, trade classification, feed quality, or order-entry control. Robinhood is therefore not assumed to reproduce the expert’s order-flow environment.

Until equivalent data is confirmed, use observable substitutes: price rejection at a level, inability to make a new low or high, relative volume, spread behavior, and confirmation on the next candle. Do not pretend that a candle wick is identical to verified order-flow absorption.

### The 60%–65% win-rate claim

The notes report an approximate 60%–65% win rate with a 1.5R–2R target. That may describe the trader’s own setup, sample, market, execution, and selection process; it is not a general expectation. If a strategy truly wins 60% with an average winner of 1.5R and an average loser of 1R, its pre-cost expectancy would be approximately +0.50R per trade:

```text
(0.60 × 1.5R) − (0.40 × 1R) = +0.50R
```

The calculation is useful as a benchmark, not as a promise. The claimed statistics must be verified using a complete sample, including losing sessions, skipped trades, slippage, commissions or other costs, and periods in which the setup performs poorly.

## 16. Final philosophy


The plan is not designed to promise that $26 will become $100, $1,000, or $10,000. No honest strategy can promise that outcome without substantial risk. The plan is designed to give the fixed capital its best chance of surviving while generating evidence about whether the operator and strategy have a durable edge.

The final operating statement is:

> **No money will be added. The account will use one liquid long equity position at a time, preferably through fractional shares. The 1% rule is waived because it is impractical at $26, but planned loss remains approximately $0.75–$1.00 per trade, daily loss approximately $1.25–$1.50, and weekly drawdown approximately $2.50–$3.00. Limit orders, liquidity filters, event avoidance, time stops, regime filters, paper validation, and a market-off switch are mandatory. Options, leverage, OTC securities, sub-$1 stocks, promotional names, averaging down, and forced trades are prohibited.**

The account’s primary edge is not a prediction model. It is the refusal to take trades that cannot be sized, executed, and evaluated safely.
